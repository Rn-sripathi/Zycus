Track BContract Redlining

Reading a vendor's contract against your own rules

An agent that reads a counterparty contract clause by clause, checks it against the Zycus playbook, drafts replacement language for what fails, and says plainly which findings it cannot stand behind.

FastAPI + React 6-step pipeline 3 deterministic tools / 3 model steps 86 tests ~6s per review
01The problem

Seven rules, eight clauses, and no margin for a missed one

Northwind's draft breaches the playbook in seven places across six clauses. A reviewer has to find every one, decide which are worth escalating, and write the replacement wording. Missing one is not a smaller version of the job done well: the clause ships.

  • Liability cap≥ 12 months of fees
  • Termination notice≥ 30 days
  • Auto-renewal notice≥ 30 days, no penalty
  • Payment termsNet 30 or longer
  • Data ownershipstays with Zycus
  • Indemnificationmutual, not one-sided
  • Governing lawneutral jurisdiction

Four of these rules turn on a number, so they can be settled by arithmetic. Three turn on what the words mean, and only those need a model. The system is built around that split.

02Architecture

Six steps, and the model only runs where it earns its place

One endpoint runs a fixed pipeline. Each step is one module, so the diagram and the directory listing are the same thing. Steps 4 and 5 fan out concurrently.

03The product

Every finding shows its working

Findings are grouped by what the reviewer should do with them, not by clause order. A card carries the arithmetic or the quoted evidence behind the verdict, so a finding can be checked without opening the contract.

04Where the AI got it wrong

Two bugs Claude wrote, and its own tests passed

Every module here was AI-generated. Both bugs below survived review and a green test suite, and were caught only by reading the product output against the sample contract.

Bug 1 — correct in each part, wrong as a whole

Two redlines on one clause, each undoing the other

Clause 1 breaches two rules at once. Claude drafted per clause-rule pair, which reads sensibly one function at a time, and produced two replacements that could not both be applied.

Redline A — auto-renewal

Fixed the notice to 30 days, and kept terminate for convenience upon 7 days.

Redline B — termination

Fixed the notice to 30 days, and kept non-renewal at least 10 days.

Paste either one and you fix a violation while shipping the other. Every test passed, because each redline was individually correct. The bug lives only in the relationship between two findings — which is what per-function tests cannot see.

Fix: drafting is grouped by clause, with every breached rule in one call, so a clause gets a single replacement satisfying all of them. A regression test asserts clause 1 yields one text containing neither 7 days nor 10 days.
Bug 2 — the code was right, the assumption wasn’t

The model is a poor judge of its own confidence

Asked for confidence handling, Claude did the obvious thing: let the model score itself and threshold on it. It never fired once. Against a deliberately vague draft:

Wording it quoted as evidenceConfidenceVerdict
"a commercially reasonable amount"0.80violation
"reasonable prior written notice, to be determined"0.90violation
"such jurisdiction as the parties may mutually determine"0.90no violation

Confidence sat at 0.8–0.9 however little the clause committed to, and a clause naming no jurisdiction at all was confidently cleared. The code was not buggy — it did what it said. The wrong assumption was about model behaviour, which is invisible in the source and only shows up when you run it on genuinely unclear input.

Fix: the model no longer judges its own certainty in either direction. Arithmetic already overrode confidence upward; a deterministic vagueness check now overrides it downward. Measured after: unchanged on the real contract at 7 auto-suggested and 0 flagged, inverted on the vague draft from 4 auto-suggested to 0, with all 5 routed to a human.
05The uncertainty decision

Confidence is not the model's to declare

The deliberate call: a finding's tier is decided in Python from what kind of evidence exists, never from the model's own estimate. That is what makes the top tier mean something.

High

A number was extracted and compared to the threshold. 7 days < 30 days is arithmetic.

Auto-suggest the redline.

Medium

No number, but the model quoted unambiguous wording and the clause states a real position.

Auto-suggest, marked for a look.

Low

The clause defers to an outside standard, or a deviation was asserted with nothing quoted to support it.

Flag for a human. No auto-suggestion.

Confidence and severity stay separate

Collapsing them into one risk score hides the two cases that matter most: certain but minor, and uncertain but serious. Net 15 payment terms are certain and still worth escalating; a vague data-rights clause is unresolved and would be serious if real.

Over-flagging is the safe error

The cost of a false flag is a reviewer glancing at a clause. The cost of a false pass is a clause nobody read, signed. The thresholds are tuned in that direction on purpose.

Silence is never approval

A clause no rule covers is reported as uncovered, not as compliant, which also surfaces gaps in the playbook itself.

Judgment, not just detection

Severity comes from the playbook's own reasoning rather than from the size of the gap. Payment terms are escalated because the rule itself says shorter terms need escalation, even though Net 15 is the smallest numeric miss in the contract. The two notice-period gaps are the largest misses proportionally and are still only negotiable, because they are procedural.

The honest limitation: the vagueness check is a phrase list, so a clause that hedges in wording it does not contain still reaches the model unguarded. It is a floor under the failure mode, not a solution to it.
